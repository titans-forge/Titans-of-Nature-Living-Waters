# Teaching with Living Waters

Living Waters introduces ecological reasoning through **15 waters, three comparative research journeys, 33 first-attempt questions, and 24 glossary terms**. Its practice questions are not a validated examination; gameplay points record participation, not mastery. Real learners have not yet established the educational effectiveness of this release.

## Choose a journey

| Journey | Waters | Question to carry between places |
|---|---|---|
| When a forest becomes water | Amazon and Tonle Sap | What makes seasonal nursery habitat accessible, including the way back out? |
| A life cycle needs a route | Lake Baikal and Danube | Can fish reach the habitats needed at different stages of life? |
| Follow the food beneath the surface | Alaska kelp coast and Galápagos | How do light, nutrients, and water movement support a food web? |

The other waters remain available for exploration: Okavango, Florida Bay, Lake Holiday, Nile, Euphrates, Ganges, Great Barrier Reef, Seto Inland Sea, and Yellow River Delta. A comparison transfers an ecological question; it does not imply identical species, seasons, or solutions.

## Run a short session

1. Introduce one ecological question and three useful glossary terms. Start with Guided mode; Field mode asks learners to infer a route from clues. Untimed controls let the learner focus on reasoning.
2. Ask for a prediction and a reason. Each water has one prediction and one later reflection question. “I am not sure yet” is a valid starting response; it preserves uncertainty for discussion.
3. Make a field record. Notice season, habitat, method, handling, and what the observation cannot establish. A **careful** record uses the encounter's appropriate handling response. A **shared** record includes the full community report. A catch alone does not prove habitat health or population abundance.
4. Discuss the reflection's changed situation after the encounter. The first response remains recorded; ask learners to explain how their thinking changed instead of treating the answer count as a grade.
5. Bring careful, shared records from both project waters to the comparative question. The three projects add three questions to the 30 venue questions. Community outcomes are fictional story consequences.

Listen for an explanation, a supporting clue, and a limitation. A strong closing prompt is: “What would you predict in a place we have not visited, and what would you measure before trusting that prediction?”

## Readability and evidence

The intended audience starts around age ten, but that suitability remains to be tested. Read guide clues aloud when useful; scientific names are reference labels, not spelling requirements. The glossary explains terms such as anadromous, littoral, bycatch, and recompression. Generated imagery and simulated encounters are illustrations, not scientific evidence. Source links support particular mechanisms and distinguish them from current local permissions.

Use [the learner playtest protocol](LEARNER_PLAYTEST.md) to check comprehension, delayed retention, control enjoyment, and reading needs before assigning a classroom effectiveness rating.

## Keep and share records

Progress is stored in the browser when storage is available. **Export journey** saves a portable JSON file containing progress, observations, technique details, and learning responses. Import shows a preview and replaces the current journey after confirmation; export a separate copy first when needed.

“Shared” means shared with the fictional community in the story. The game does not send a teacher an email or upload those observations. It requires no learner account or analytics service. Someone who receives an exported file can read the included responses. Use participant codes in any separate classroom notes, and keep control of exported files. Opening a source link visits the authority's external website.
